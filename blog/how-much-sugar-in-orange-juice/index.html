How Much Sugar Is in Orange Juice? A Glass vs. a Whole Orange

An 8 oz glass of orange juice has about 21 g of sugar, close to 5 sugar cubes. See every glass size, how it compares with a whole orange and soda, and easy swaps.

By the Pipgrove team. November 18, 2026, 5 min read.

An 8 oz (1 cup) glass of 100% orange juice has about 21 grams of sugar, according to USDA nutrient data. That is close to 5 sugar cubes. None of it is "added" on a US label, but your body meets it in a very similar way to the sugar in a soft drink: fast, with almost no fiber, and without filling you up much.

That does not make orange juice a bad food. It does make it worth knowing how much is in the glass you actually pour, which is often bigger than 8 oz.

Orange juice sugar by glass size

USDA FoodData Central lists 100% orange juice at about 8.4 g of sugar per 100 g. That makes common sizes easy to work out:

Serving Sugar Sugar cubes
4 oz (small juice glass) about 10 g about 2.5
8 oz (1 cup) about 21 g about 5
12 oz about 31 g about 8
16 oz (to-go bottle) about 42 g about 10
1 medium whole orange about 12 g about 3

Brands vary a little with the oranges used, and "pulp" or "no pulp" barely changes it. Orange drinks, "juice cocktails" and "orange-flavored beverages" are a different product: they are mostly water and added sugar, so check the label for the added sugar line.

Juice vs. a whole orange: why it is not the same

A medium orange has roughly 12 g of sugar and around 3 g of fiber. A glass of juice takes the sugar of two or three oranges and leaves most of the fiber behind. Three things change:

  • Speed. You can drink a glass of juice in under a minute. Eating three oranges takes a while, and most people stop after one.
  • Fullness. Liquid calories tend to fill you up less than the same calories from food, so juice rarely replaces anything else you eat.
  • Fiber. The fiber in whole fruit slows how quickly the sugar reaches your blood.

This is why health bodies treat juice and fruit differently. The World Health Organization counts the sugar in fruit juice, even fresh-squeezed, as free sugar and suggests keeping free sugars under 10% of daily calories, ideally under 5% (about 25 g a day for most adults). The sugar inside whole fruit is not part of that limit.

The US Dietary Guidelines say 100% juice can count toward your fruit intake, but at least half of your fruit should come from whole fruit. Large observational studies point the same way: a 2013 analysis of about 187,000 US health professionals (Muraki and colleagues, BMJ) linked eating more whole fruit with a lower risk of type 2 diabetes and drinking more fruit juice with a slightly higher risk. That kind of study cannot prove cause, but the pattern is consistent.

Is "no sugar added" orange juice lower in sugar?

No. "No sugar added" only means nothing was added on top of the juice. All 100% orange juice is "no sugar added" by nature, and it still has about 21 g per cup. "Reduced sugar" or "lighter" juices usually get there by adding water, which also cuts the vitamin C per glass.

What about kids?

The American Academy of Pediatrics suggests no juice for babies under 1, up to 4 oz a day for ages 1 to 3, 4 to 6 oz for ages 4 to 6, and up to 8 oz for ages 7 to 18. It also recommends serving juice in a cup with a meal rather than sipping it all day. Whole fruit is the better everyday choice for kids too.

Easy ways to keep juice without the sugar load

You do not need to give up orange juice. A few small changes cut the sugar a lot:

  1. Use a small glass. A 4 oz juice glass has half the sugar of a full cup, and it still tastes like breakfast.
  2. Cut it with sparkling water. Half juice, half sparkling water gives you a fizzy orange drink with half the sugar.
  3. Eat the orange instead. Peel one, or add orange segments to yogurt or oatmeal.
  4. Have juice with food, not alone. Drinking it with eggs, yogurt or nuts at breakfast blunts the sugar hit compared with juice on an empty stomach.
  5. Swap the to-go bottle. A 16 oz bottle of juice is the easiest 40 g of sugar of the day to miss. Water with a squeeze of orange or unsweetened iced tea are good stand-ins.

When to talk to a doctor

If you have diabetes, prediabetes or gestational diabetes, ask your doctor or a dietitian how juice fits your plan. Juice raises blood sugar quickly, which is also why it is used to treat lows. Talk to a doctor too if you notice strong thirst, frequent urination, or unexplained tiredness or weight loss. This article is general information, not medical advice.

The short version

  • An 8 oz glass of orange juice has about 21 g of sugar, roughly 5 sugar cubes. A 16 oz bottle has about 42 g.
  • It is not "added sugar" on a US label, but the WHO counts juice sugar as free sugar.
  • A whole orange has about 12 g of sugar plus fiber, and fills you up more.
  • Small glass, half sparkling water, or the whole fruit: any of these cuts the sugar without giving up the taste.

Quick answers

How much sugar is in a glass of orange juice?

An 8 oz (1 cup) glass of 100% orange juice has about 21 g of sugar, based on USDA data. That is roughly 5 sugar cubes.

Is the sugar in orange juice added sugar?

Not on a US label. Sugar that is naturally in 100% juice is not listed as added sugar. But the World Health Organization counts the sugar in all fruit juice, even fresh-squeezed, as free sugar, the kind it suggests keeping low.

Does orange juice have more sugar than soda?

Ounce for ounce, orange juice has a bit less sugar than regular cola, about 21 g per 8 oz versus 26 g. But a typical 12 to 16 oz glass of juice lands close to a can of soda.

Is a whole orange better than orange juice?

For sugar, usually yes. A medium orange has about 12 g of sugar plus around 3 g of fiber, takes longer to eat and fills you up more. A glass of juice packs the sugar of two or three oranges with almost no fiber.
