How to Stop Sugar Cravings: 11 Things That Actually Help

Practical, research-backed ways to stop sugar cravings in the moment and make them rarer over time, from a 15-minute walk to riding the urge and fixing sleep.

By the Pipgrove team. November 7, 2026, 5 min read.

A sugar craving feels urgent. It tells you that you need something sweet right now, and that it will not stop until you get it. The good news is that it does stop, usually much sooner than it promises to. This guide covers what helps in the moment, what makes cravings rarer over time, and how to handle a slip without starting from zero.

Part one: what to do when the craving hits

1. Name it and let it peak

The urge to eat something sweet behaves like a wave: it builds, peaks and then fades. Psychologists call the skill of staying with it without acting urge surfing. In a small study of regular chocolate eaters who carried a bag of chocolates for a week, people taught a brief acceptance exercise of this kind ate fewer chocolates than a control group, even though their cravings were just as frequent (Hulbert-Williams et al., Journal of Health Psychology).

That last part matters. The goal is not to stop the thought of chocolate from ever showing up. It is to notice it, call it a craving, and let it pass without obeying it.

Try it: breathe in for four seconds and out for six, and notice where you feel the urge in your body. You can do a 30-second version on our home page.

2. Take a 15-minute walk

Movement is one of the best-tested ways to take the edge off. In a workplace simulation, regular chocolate eaters who walked briskly for 15 minutes before working ate about half as much chocolate from a bowl on their desk as people who rested, roughly 15 g against 28 g (Oh and Taylor, Appetite, 2011). If you cannot get outside, a few flights of stairs works too.

3. Drink a big glass of water

Thirst and hunger signals are easy to mix up, and a glass of water gives your hands and mouth something to do while the urge passes. It is not magic, but it buys you the few minutes that a craving needs to fade.

4. Change the room

Cravings are tied to places and routines: the couch after dinner, the office kitchen at 3 pm. Stepping into a different room, brushing your teeth or starting a shower breaks the cue. Brushing your teeth has a bonus: almost nothing tastes good right after mint.

5. Make a swap that is still sweet

You do not have to fight every sweet craving with celery. A bowl of berries, an apple with cinnamon, frozen grapes, two dates with a few nuts, or Greek yogurt with fruit all scratch the itch with far less added sugar. Many people also say fruit starts to taste sweeter after a few weeks of cutting back.

6. Decide in advance

When a craving hits, your brain is a poor negotiator. Make the decision earlier with an if-then plan: "If I want something sweet after dinner, then I will make a cup of tea and walk around the block." Writing the plan down in a specific form makes it far more likely that you will follow it.

Part two: how to have fewer cravings

7. Protect your sleep

Short sleep changes what food looks like to your brain. In a study at UC Berkeley, one night of sleep deprivation increased people's desire for high-calorie foods and dulled activity in the brain regions that weigh decisions (Greer, Goldstein and Walker, Nature Communications, 2013). If you are fighting cravings every afternoon, look at your bedtime first.

8. Eat real meals with protein

Long gaps between meals, or meals that are mostly refined carbs, set you up for a 4 pm slump that sugar seems to fix. Build meals around protein, fibre and some fat (eggs, beans, fish, yogurt, vegetables, whole grains) and the slump gets smaller.

9. Know your craving times

Most people have one or two danger zones a day: mid-afternoon at work, after dinner, late at night. Once you know yours, you can plan for it, with a walk scheduled at 3 pm or a tea ready after dinner. 10. Clear the easy reach

If cookies are on the counter, you will eat cookies. Keep sweets out of sight or out of the house for the first few weeks, and keep the swaps (fruit, nuts, tea) where the sweets used to be.

11. Watch the hidden sugar

Sauces, granola, flavoured yogurt, bread and "healthy" bars often carry a surprising amount of added sugar, which keeps the taste for sweetness switched on. On a label, look for added sugars. Four grams is about one sugar cube. For a sense of scale, the American Heart Association suggests at most about 25 g of added sugar a day for women and 36 g for men (Healthwise summary of AHA guidance).

If you slip

A slip is information, not failure. Ask what led to it (tired, stressed, bored, a party) and write down what you will do next time. The days you already managed still count. That is how we built Pipgrove: your garden never loses the days you earned, and a slip simply becomes part of the plan.

When to talk to a doctor

If you have diabetes, a history of disordered eating, or cravings that feel out of control, talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian before changing how you eat. Our health note lists support lines.

The short version

  • In the moment: name the craving, breathe slowly, walk for 15 minutes, drink water, change the room.
  • Keep sweet swaps ready: berries, an apple with cinnamon, yogurt with fruit, tea.
  • Over time: sleep more, eat real meals, plan for your craving times, keep sweets out of easy reach.

Quick answers

How long does a sugar craving last?

A craving usually rises, peaks and then fades on its own, often within minutes, especially if you change what you are doing. Riding it out with slow breathing or a short walk is a good test of that.

What can I eat to stop sugar cravings?

Fruit such as berries or an apple, Greek yogurt with fruit, a handful of nuts, or a cup of cinnamon or fruit tea all work as swaps. Pairing something sweet with protein or fat tends to keep you full longer.

Why are my sugar cravings worse at night?

Evening is when tiredness, stress and habit pile up. Short sleep makes high-calorie food more appealing the next day, and an after-dinner dessert routine trains the brain to expect sugar at that time.
