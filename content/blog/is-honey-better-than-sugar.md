Honey has a healthy reputation. It is natural, it comes from bees, and it looks a lot nicer in a jar than a bag of white sugar. So it is fair to ask: if you are trying to cut back on sugar, is swapping to honey a real upgrade?

The short answer: honey is slightly different from sugar, not meaningfully better. Below are the actual numbers, what health organizations say, and the situations where choosing honey can still make sense.

## Honey vs sugar: the numbers

Here is a rough side-by-side using typical USDA values. Exact numbers vary by honey type and how full your spoon is.

| Per level tablespoon | Honey | White sugar |
|---|---|---|
| Weight | about 21 g | about 12.5 g |
| Calories | about 64 | about 48 |
| Sugar | about 17 g | about 12.5 g |
| Water | about 17% | almost 0% |

A tablespoon of honey is heavier than a tablespoon of sugar, because honey is dense. So **spoon for spoon, honey carries more sugar and more calories**. Gram for gram it is a bit lower, because some of honey is water.

What about sweetness? Honey is mostly fructose and glucose, and fructose tastes sweeter than table sugar. Many people find they can use a little less honey than sugar for the same taste. That small difference is the main practical advantage, and it only helps if you actually use less.

## What is in honey besides sugar?

Honey contains small amounts of antioxidants (polyphenols), traces of minerals and some enzymes. Darker honeys like buckwheat tend to have more than light ones.

The catch is quantity. A teaspoon or two of honey in your tea gives you a very small dose of these compounds, far less than you would get from a handful of berries, a cup of tea itself or a serving of vegetables. Honey is not a meaningful source of vitamins or minerals at normal amounts.

## How health organizations treat honey

This is where the answer gets clear.

- The [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241549028) defines **free sugars** as sugars added to foods plus the sugars naturally present in honey, syrups and fruit juices. It recommends keeping free sugars under 10% of daily energy, ideally under 5%. Honey is in the same bucket as white sugar.
- The [American Heart Association](https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/healthy-eating/eat-smart/sugar/how-much-sugar-is-too-much) suggests keeping added sugar to about 25 g a day for most women and 36 g for most men. Honey you stir in counts toward that.
- On US nutrition labels, honey added to a product appears under **Added Sugars**.

## Blood sugar: honey vs sugar

You will often read that honey has a lower glycemic index than sugar. Published values for honey vary widely by type, and many land in a similar range to table sugar. In real life the difference is small, and what you eat honey with (yogurt, oats, toast) matters more than the honey itself.

If you manage diabetes, treat honey as you would any other sugar and follow the plan you have with your care team.

## When honey can make sense

Honey is not a health food, but it is not a villain either. It can be a reasonable choice when:

- **You want flavor, not just sweetness.** A strong honey on plain yogurt or oats gives a lot of taste from a small drizzle, so you may end up using less than with sugar.
- **You are cutting down step by step.** Going from two sugars in tea to one teaspoon of honey, then half, is a perfectly good path.
- **You simply enjoy it.** Enjoying a little honey on purpose is very different from sugar sneaking into your day from drinks and snacks.

What does not work: switching to honey and keeping the same number of spoons, or adding honey to things you never used to sweeten because it feels "healthy".

## Honey and other "natural" sugars

The same logic applies to maple syrup, agave, coconut sugar, brown sugar and date syrup. Each has its own taste and small differences, but your body handles them mostly as sugar, and WHO counts syrups as free sugars too. Agave is very high in fructose, which is not an advantage.

The swap that changes things is not one sugar for another. It is **less sweetness overall**, with flavor coming from fruit, cinnamon, vanilla and other spices. Your taste adapts over a few weeks, as described in [how long it takes to stop craving sugar](/blog/how-long-to-stop-craving-sugar/).

## The one firm rule: no honey for babies

Children under 12 months should not have honey in any form, including in baked goods made with raw honey or on a pacifier. Honey can contain spores that cause infant botulism, a rare but serious illness. Health authorities like the CDC and the American Academy of Pediatrics are clear on this.

## When to talk to a doctor

If you have diabetes, prediabetes or another condition affected by sugar, are pregnant, or have questions about feeding a young child, talk to your doctor, pediatrician or a registered dietitian. This article is general information, not medical advice.

## The short version

- Spoon for spoon, honey has more sugar and calories than white sugar.
- Its antioxidants and minerals are real but tiny at normal amounts.
- WHO and US labels count honey as free or added sugar, same as table sugar.
- It can still help if its stronger flavor lets you use less.
- Never give honey to babies under 1 year.
