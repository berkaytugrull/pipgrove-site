Milk is one of those foods that surprises people when they start reading labels. You pick up a carton of plain milk, look at the Nutrition Facts, and see **12 grams of sugar** in a single cup. That can feel alarming if you are trying to cut back on sugar.

Here is the short answer: a cup of plain milk has about 12 g of sugar, and none of it is added. It is **lactose**, the sugar that is naturally in milk. Flavored milks and some plant milks are a different story. Below are the numbers for the most common options, what they mean, and where the real added sugar hides.

## How much sugar is in milk, per cup

According to USDA FoodData Central, an 8 oz (244 g) cup of plain cow's milk has about 12 grams of sugar, and that barely changes with the fat level:

| Milk (1 cup) | Sugar | Added | Cubes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole milk | ~12 g | 0 g | ~3 |
| 2% milk | ~12 g | 0 g | ~3 |
| Skim milk | ~12 g | 0 g | ~3 |
| Lactose-free milk | ~12 g | 0 g | ~3 |
| Chocolate milk | ~24 g | ~11 g | ~6 |
| Oat milk, original | ~7 g | some | ~2 |
| Almond, sweetened | ~7 g | ~7 g | ~2 |
| Almond, unsweetened | 0 g | 0 g | 0 |
| Soy, unsweetened | ~1 g | 0 g | 0 |

Cup = 8 oz. "Added" is added sugar. Plant milk values are typical US label numbers and change from brand to brand, so check the carton you actually buy. One sugar cube is about 4 grams.

## What is lactose?

Lactose is the main sugar in milk from cows, goats, sheep and people. It is made of two smaller sugars, glucose and galactose, joined together. It tastes only mildly sweet, which is why plain milk does not taste like a sweet drink even though it has 12 grams of sugar.

In lactose-free milk, an enzyme called lactase is added to split lactose into glucose and galactose ahead of time. The total sugar stays about the same, but the milk tastes noticeably sweeter. That sweeter taste is not a sign of added sugar.

## Does the sugar in milk count as added sugar?

No. Health guidelines draw a clear line here:

- **The FDA** requires US labels to show "Includes Xg Added Sugars" under total sugars. For plain milk that line reads 0 g, because lactose is naturally present.
- **The World Health Organization** recommends keeping *free sugars* under 10% of daily energy, and ideally under 5%. Free sugars include sugar added to foods plus the sugar in honey, syrups and fruit juice. The WHO definition specifically leaves out sugars naturally present in milk.
- **The American Heart Association** suggests no more than about 25 g of added sugar a day for most women and 36 g for most men. Lactose in plain milk does not count toward this.

So if you drink plain milk, you are not "using up" your added sugar budget. If you want the bigger picture, see [added sugar vs. natural sugar](/blog/added-sugar-vs-natural-sugar/) and [how much sugar per day](/blog/how-much-sugar-per-day/).

## Where the added sugar in milk actually hides

The sugar to watch for is in the flavored and sweetened versions:

- **Chocolate and strawberry milk.** A cup of low-fat chocolate milk has roughly double the sugar of plain milk. About half comes from the milk itself and the rest is added.
- **Coffee shop milk drinks.** A latte made with plain milk only has milk sugar. Once you add syrup, a flavored latte or mocha can carry 25 to 40 grams of added sugar.
- **"Original" plant milks.** Many oat, almond, rice and soy milks labeled "original" are lightly sweetened. The unsweetened version of the same brand usually has 0 to 1 gram.
- **Oat milk.** Oat milk often lists sugar even when no sugar is added. During processing, enzymes break some of the oat starch into sugars such as maltose. Some brands count part of this as added sugar on the label, others do not, so look at both lines.
- **Milk-based shakes and protein drinks.** These range widely, from almost none to more than a can of soda. The label is the only reliable guide.

## How to read a milk label quickly

1. **Find "Includes Xg Added Sugars."** This tells you what was put in on top of the natural milk sugar.
2. **Check the ingredients.** Words like cane sugar, sugar, dextrose, syrup or "evaporated cane juice" mean sweetener was added.
3. **Compare serving sizes.** Most US labels use 1 cup (8 oz), but single-serve bottles are often 12 to 14 oz.
4. **Pick "unsweetened" for plant milks.** "Original" and "plain" often still contain sugar.

## Easy swaps if you like flavored milk

- Mix half chocolate milk with half plain milk. It still tastes like chocolate milk with about a quarter less sugar.
- Stir unsweetened cocoa and a little cinnamon into warm milk instead of a packet mix.
- Try a smaller pump or a "half sweet" order at the coffee shop.
- Switch from sweetened to unsweetened plant milk gradually, mixing the two for a week or two while your taste adjusts.

## When to talk to a doctor

If you have diabetes, ask your doctor or a dietitian how milk fits your carbohydrate plan, because lactose still raises blood sugar like any carbohydrate. Talk to a doctor too if milk regularly gives you bloating, cramps or diarrhea, since that can point to lactose intolerance or another digestive issue worth checking. For babies and young children, follow your pediatrician's advice on which milk to use and when. This article is general information, not medical advice.

## The short version

- A cup of plain milk, whole or skim, has about 12 g of sugar, roughly 3 sugar cubes.
- That sugar is lactose. It is not added sugar and does not count toward WHO or AHA added sugar limits.
- Chocolate milk has about double the sugar, and about half of it is added.
- For plant milks, "unsweetened" is the version with little or no sugar.
