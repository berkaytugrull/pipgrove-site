"Natural sugar" and "added sugar" show up on labels, in headlines and in diet advice, and it is easy to come away thinking one is good and one is poison. The truth is calmer and more useful. Here is what the difference really is, why it matters, and how to spot each one in your kitchen.

## The short answer

**Natural sugar** is the sugar that is already inside a whole food: fructose in an apple, lactose in plain milk, a little sugar in carrots or tomatoes.

**Added sugar** is sugar put into a food or drink during processing, cooking or at the table: the sugar in soda, cookies, flavoured yogurt, ketchup, sweetened coffee, and the spoon you stir into tea.

Chemically, your body breaks both down into the same few simple sugars. The difference is not the molecule. It is the **package** the sugar comes in and **how much** of it you end up eating.

## Why the package matters

A medium apple has a decent amount of sugar, but it also has water, fibre and a lot of chewing. It takes a few minutes to eat, it fills you up, and very few people eat four in a row.

A can of regular cola has around 39 g of sugar, close to ten teaspoons, with no fibre and nothing to chew. You can drink it in two minutes and still feel ready for lunch. You can see what that looks like in cubes in our post on [how much sugar is in a Coke](/blog/how-much-sugar-in-coke/).

That is why health groups set limits for added sugar and not for whole fruit. Fibre, water and protein slow you down and make it hard to overdo it. Added sugar slips in easily, often in foods that do not even taste very sweet.

## What counts as added sugar

On US food labels, "added sugars" includes sugars and syrups added during processing, sugar from honey and syrups, and sugar from concentrated fruit or vegetable juice beyond what you would expect from the same amount of plain juice ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-facts-label/added-sugars-nutrition-facts-label)). In plain terms, all of these count as added when they go into a food:

- White and brown sugar, cane sugar, raw sugar
- Honey, maple syrup, agave nectar, coconut sugar, date syrup
- Corn syrup, high-fructose corn syrup, rice syrup, malt syrup
- Anything ending in **-ose** on an ingredient list: sucrose, dextrose, maltose, glucose
- Fruit juice concentrate used as a sweetener

"Raw", "organic" and "natural" on the front of the pack do not change this. Organic cane sugar is still added sugar.

## Where the WHO draws the line

The World Health Organization uses a slightly wider term, **free sugars**: all sugars added to foods plus the sugars naturally present in honey, syrups and fruit juices. It recommends keeping free sugars below 10% of your daily energy, and ideally below 5% ([WHO guideline, 2015](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241549028)).

The practical difference is fruit juice. Even 100% juice with nothing added counts as free sugar for the WHO, because juicing removes most of the fibre and makes it easy to drink a lot. A glass of orange juice is not the same as eating an orange.

## How much added sugar is too much?

The American Heart Association suggests no more than about **25 g a day for most women and 36 g for most men** ([AHA](https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/healthy-eating/eat-smart/sugar/added-sugars)). The Dietary Guidelines for Americans say to keep added sugars below 10% of daily calories.

There is no similar limit for sugar in whole fruit, vegetables or plain milk. If you want to see where your day lands, our [daily sugar calculator](/blog/how-much-sugar-per-day/) adds it up in a few taps.

## How to read a label in 20 seconds

1. **Find the Nutrition Facts panel.** Under "Total Sugars" there is a line that says "Includes Xg Added Sugars". That number is the one that counts toward your limit.
2. **Compare it with the limit.** 12 g of added sugar in a yogurt is about half of the 25 g suggestion for women.
3. **Scan the ingredient list.** Ingredients are listed by weight. If sugar or a syrup is in the first three, sugar is a main ingredient. Several different sweeteners spread through the list is another clue.
4. **Ignore front-of-pack words.** "No refined sugar", "naturally sweetened" and "made with real fruit" do not mean low in added sugar.

Outside the US, labels often show only total sugars. Then the ingredient list is your best tool: a plain yogurt with only milk and cultures has natural sugar only; a fruit yogurt with "sugar" or "fruit concentrate" listed has added sugar too.

## Common mix-ups

**"Fruit has too much sugar."** Whole fruit is one of the easiest ways to handle a sweet craving, and it does not count toward added sugar limits. If you have diabetes, your care team may give you personal guidance on portions. For everyone else, fruit is a friend. See [what to eat when craving sugar](/blog/what-to-eat-when-craving-sugar/) for ideas.

**"Honey is the healthy sugar."** Honey has a few trace compounds, but in the amounts people eat it behaves much like table sugar and counts as added sugar.

**"Dried fruit is the same as fresh."** It is still whole fruit with fibre, but without the water it is very easy to eat a lot. Keep it to a small handful.

**"Sugar-free means no sugar at all."** Sugar-free products may use sweeteners instead. That is a separate topic; they do not count as added sugar on labels.

## A simple way to use this

You do not need to fear sugar or track every gram. A calm approach works better:

- Keep whole fruit, vegetables and plain dairy as they are.
- Cut back on the big sources of added sugar first, usually sweet drinks, sweets and sweetened breakfast foods.
- Swap one item a day: sparkling water for soda, plain yogurt plus berries for fruit yogurt.

## When to talk to a doctor

If you have diabetes, prediabetes or take medication that affects blood sugar, ask your doctor or a dietitian how sugar from fruit and drinks fits your plan. The same goes if you have a history of disordered eating, where label reading can sometimes become stressful rather than helpful.
