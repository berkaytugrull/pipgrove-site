Brown sugar looks more natural than white sugar. It is darker, it smells of caramel, and it often sits next to words like "raw" or "unrefined" on the shelf. So when people try to cut back on sugar, a common first move is to switch to brown and feel a little better about it.

The honest answer: brown sugar and white sugar are close cousins. One is not a health upgrade over the other. Here is what actually differs, with numbers, and the few situations where the choice does matter.

## How brown and white sugar are made

Both usually start as juice from sugarcane or sugar beets. The juice is boiled down until sugar crystals form, and the crystals are spun away from a dark syrup called **molasses**.

- **White sugar** is those crystals washed and refined until almost nothing but sucrose is left.
- **Most brown sugar** in supermarkets is white sugar with a little molasses mixed back in. Light brown sugar has less, dark brown has more.
- **Less refined sugars** such as turbinado, demerara and muscovado keep some of their original molasses instead of having it added back. Muscovado keeps the most, which is why it is dark, sticky and strongly flavored.

In every case, the main ingredient is the same molecule: sucrose. Brown sugar is roughly 95 to 97% sugar; white sugar is close to 100%.

## Brown sugar vs white sugar: the numbers

Here is a rough side-by-side for one level teaspoon, based on typical USDA values. Exact numbers depend on the brand and how tightly you pack the spoon.

| Per level teaspoon (about 4 g) | Brown sugar | White sugar |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | about 15 | about 16 |
| Sugar | about 3.9 g | about 4 g |
| Calcium | about 3 mg | 0 mg |
| Potassium | about 5 mg | almost 0 mg |
| Iron | under 0.1 mg | 0 mg |

For a sense of scale: a cup of milk has around 300 mg of calcium, and a banana around 400 mg of potassium. To get meaningful minerals from brown sugar you would need to eat amounts no one would recommend.

One practical twist: recipes usually ask for brown sugar to be **packed**. A packed cup of brown sugar weighs more than a cup of loose white sugar, so spoon for spoon it can carry a little more sugar, not less.

## Does brown sugar affect blood sugar differently?

Not in a way you would notice. Both are mostly sucrose, which your body splits into glucose and fructose. Published glycemic index values for the two land in a similar range. What you eat the sugar with, such as fiber, protein or fat, has a much bigger effect on how your blood sugar responds than the color of the sugar.

If you live with diabetes or prediabetes, brown sugar counts exactly like white sugar.

## What health organizations say

Health guidelines do not separate sugars by color.

- The [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241549028) counts all sugars added to food and drinks as **free sugars**, along with honey, syrups and fruit juice, and recommends keeping them under 10% of daily energy, ideally under 5%.
- The [American Heart Association](https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/healthy-eating/eat-smart/sugar/how-much-sugar-is-too-much) suggests about 25 g of added sugar a day for most women and 36 g for most men. Brown, white, raw or coconut, it all goes in the same budget.
- On US nutrition labels, brown sugar used in a product is listed under **Added Sugars**.

## Where the choice does matter: taste and cooking

Brown and white sugar are not interchangeable in the kitchen, and that is the real reason to pick one over the other.

- **Flavor.** Molasses gives brown sugar a caramel, toffee-like taste. In coffee, oatmeal or a sauce, a small amount of brown sugar can taste "sweeter" and richer than the same amount of white, which may help you use a little less.
- **Moisture.** Brown sugar holds water. Cookies made with brown sugar come out softer and chewier; cookies made with white sugar spread more and turn crisp.
- **Color.** Brown sugar darkens baked goods and sauces faster.

If you bake at home and want less sugar overall, many recipes still work well with a quarter less sugar than written. Spices like cinnamon, vanilla and cardamom help fill the gap.

## What about turbinado, demerara, muscovado and coconut sugar?

These are often sold as the "natural" options. They do have more character: bigger crystals, deeper flavor, a bit more molasses. Nutritionally, they sit in the same place as brown sugar. Coconut sugar is also mostly sucrose and counts as added sugar too.

The same logic applies to honey, which we break down in [is honey better than sugar?](/blog/is-honey-better-than-sugar/). Choosing a different sugar changes the taste. Using **less sweetness overall** is what changes your intake.

## A calmer way to think about it

If you love brown sugar on your oatmeal, you do not need to give it up or feel bad about it. A few practical moves work better than switching sugar types:

1. **Measure once.** Use a real teaspoon for a few days to see how much you actually add.
2. **Cut by a quarter.** Go from two teaspoons to one and a half. Most people barely notice after a week.
3. **Let flavor do some work.** Cinnamon on oatmeal, vanilla in coffee, ripe fruit in yogurt.
4. **Look at drinks first.** For most people, sweetened drinks add far more sugar than the spoon at breakfast. See [how much sugar is in a Coke](/blog/how-much-sugar-in-coke/) for a quick reality check.

Your taste for sweetness adapts over a few weeks of using less, as described in [how long it takes to stop craving sugar](/blog/how-long-to-stop-craving-sugar/).

## When to talk to a doctor

If you have diabetes, prediabetes, kidney disease or another condition where sugar or minerals like potassium matter, or if you are pregnant, talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian before making big changes. This article is general information, not medical advice.

## The short version

- Most brown sugar is white sugar with a little molasses added back.
- Calories, sugar and blood sugar effects are nearly the same.
- The extra minerals are real but tiny: a few milligrams per teaspoon.
- Both count as added sugar in WHO and AHA guidance.
- Pick by taste and recipe, then focus on using less overall.
